# Snap, Diff, Confirm: Building Your Fridge Tracker

Build Phase 1 as a small household web app (a PWA), separate from Cut Protocol but on its React, Vite and Tailwind stack, backed by a second free Supabase project whose single server-side function sends each scan's zone-labelled phone photos and the current inventory list to a cheap vision model and gets back JSON in a fixed format; **Claude Haiku 5.5, at US$0.10/$0.50 per million input/output tokens**, is the default model to try. Your own code, not the model, then decides what is new, what is gone and how many days each item has sat there, because vision models name visible groceries well but miscount hidden items and struggle to recognise the same object across two photos. That is exactly where earlier products broke: Samsung's camera fridges could not see door bins, drawers or the freezer, made owners hold each item up and wait for a beep, and for most of their life needed expiry dates typed in, while the Smarter FridgeCam recognised nothing in two weeks of testing and Liebherr's HNGRYnsite camera stopped working when its app shut down. Money is not the constraint (AI calls should cost about **C$0.20–0.75 a month** and hosting **C$0–7**); keeping the list current is. So the design rests on a few seconds of scanning per zone as groceries go away, a list of changes accepted by default with one-tap fixes, no typed dates (first-seen dates plus USDA FoodKeeper and Health Canada storage times produce "spinach, day 9, check it"), and treating an item a scan missed as "not sure" rather than "gone". Phase 2 should be a USB-powered ESP32-S3 or Raspberry Pi camera (roughly **C$80–185 each**) that shoots after the door closes and feeds the same pipeline as one more scan source, but never the system of record, because no single fixed camera sees a whole fridge. The sensible order is to test models on 30–50 labelled photos of your own fridge first, then build scanning, change detection, review and the store view, live with it for a month, and only then pick up the soldering iron.

## Ten years of fridge cameras converged on what your phone already does

Two kinds of in-fridge camera have reached the market. The modest kind photographs the shelves when the door closes so you can check them from the store. Bosch announced it at IFA 2015 and still offers it on some high-end models ([Reviewed](https://reviewed.com/refrigerators/news/this-app-shows-you-whats-in-your-fridge); [Bosch UK](https://www.bosch-home.co.uk/bosch-innovations/homeconnect/homeconnect-fridges)). The ambitious kind is Samsung's AI Vision Inside, a camera at the top of the cabinet that logs food going in and out. For years it recognised only **33 fresh foods, later 37 plus about 50 pre-registered packaged goods** ([Samsung Canada](https://www.samsung.com/ca/refrigerators/family-hub/); [Samsung US Newsroom](https://news.samsung.com/us/samsung-family-hub-2025-update-elevates-smart-home-ecosystem/)). It records nothing put in the FlexZone drawer, the freezer or either door ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/home/kitchen-tech/samsung-bespoke-fridge-with-ai-review-all-the-bells-and-whistles-140000099.html)). Owners must **hold each item up and wait for a beep**, and putting something in a door pocket makes the fridge think it was removed ([Trusted Reviews](https://www.trustedreviews.com/reviews/samsung-bespoke-ai-4-door-french-door-rf90f29aecraa)). Samsung's own documentation says expiry dates "should be manually entered by users via the SmartThings app" ([Samsung](https://www.samsung.com/global/sustainability/popup/popup_doc/AYUr58faFhIAIx_C/)). Reviewed concluded the camera "might be more work than it's worth" ([Reviewed](https://www.reviewed.com/refrigerators/content/samsung-bespoke-refrigerator-rf29db9900qd-review)).

In May 2026 Samsung began rolling out an update, starting with select US models, that adds cloud recognition by Google's Gemini model. Engadget reports the fridge now recognises **more than 2,000 foods**, tells Diet Coke from Coke Zero, and warns as perishables near the end of their shelf life **based on how long they have been stored**. The feature needs an active Wi-Fi connection ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2167892/samsungs-bespoke-update-is-big-step-towards-a-useful-ai-for-your-fridge/)). Techlicious gave Gemini a phone photo of a cupboard holding about 30 partly hidden items and got a "nearly complete accounting". It called the US$3,399 fridge a pricey way to get what a phone already does ([Techlicious](https://www.techlicious.com/blog/samsung-bespoke-fridge-vision-ai-with-google-gemini/)). The market leader's 2026 answer is, in effect, your Phase 1.

Retrofit cameras fared worse. Smarter FridgeCam, Fridge Eye and Liebherr's HNGRYnsite shared one design: a battery camera inside the fridge that took a photo when the door closed and uploaded it to the vendor's cloud. Expert Reviews found FridgeCam recognised **no items at all** over a couple of weeks. Smarter said accuracy would improve as more users tagged items ([Expert Reviews](https://www.expertreviews.co.uk/home-garden/large-appliances/smarter-fridgecam-review)), and retailers now list the camera as discontinued ([Mobile Fun](https://www.mobilefun.com/smarter-fridgecam-wifi-enabled-food-tracking-camera-85610)). HNGRYnsite cameras sold for €149.90 ([Liebherr](https://www.liebherr.com/en-us/n/always-know-what%E2%80%99s-inside-with-the-fridge-camera-hngrynsite-powered-by-liebherr-27846-3782185)). When Liebherr shut down the HNGRY app on **31 March 2025**, the cameras lost all function, with no local access to images and no firmware to repurpose them ([Consumer Rights Wiki](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/HNGRYnsite_Camera:_From_smart_kitchen_aid_to_e-waste)). Hisense's own R&D, as reported by an RFID vendor, concluded that cameras "can only be used as a supplement" because of blocked views and condensation from door openings ([Seiko RFID](https://www.seikorfid.com/news/RFID-ingredient-management-refrigerator.html)). The one feature reviewers consistently valued was a quick look at the shelves from the store.

Phone apps made it easy to get items into a list but never solved getting them out. Barcode trackers need a "consume" tap for everything, and apps that import receipts see purchases but not consumption. A Galaxus writer dropped the self-hosted inventory app Grocy after about a week. He called it "semi-professional warehouse logistics" and concluded that "the effort involved simply outweighs the benefits" ([Galaxus](https://www.galaxus.de/en/page/how-i-want-to-digitise-the-contents-of-my-fridge-and-fail-to-do-so-39565)). A small pilot study of two food-waste apps, published in JMIR Formative Research, found **no measurable effect** on household food waste, and participants blamed "too many manual operations" ([JMIR Formative Research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9482070/)). Syncing with store accounts is fragile even where it exists. Cooklist links to US grocers' loyalty cards and rates **4.7 on iOS but about 3.2 on Google Play**, where users report store logins failing ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/cooklist-pantry-meals-recipes/id1352600944); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.cooklist.android&hl=en_US)).

The 2025–26 wave of DIY builds teaches the same lesson. Jan Dubovsky's MIT-licensed fridge-core uses an ESP32-CAM board with a magnetic door switch and its built-in flash LED. It sends a 640×480 snapshot through Home Assistant's AI Task feature into an editable to-do list. Its guide calls the result "an AI-assisted inventory that you can correct, not a database you can trust without checking" ([PopularAI](https://www.popularai.org/p/diy-ai-fridge-camera-home-assistant)). The companion fridge-card, at v1.17.0 as of 8 October 2026, lets you mark items eaten and **stops the AI from overwriting any field a person has edited** ([fridge-card](https://github.com/jan-tdy/fridge-card)). That is the most transferable idea in the survey. One builder who wrote up a "scan your fridge" app found that "item parsing worked fine"; the hard part was correcting the list and then scanning again ([Medium](https://medium.com/@andytlim/every-vibe-coder-eventually-builds-the-scan-your-fridge-app-8827dde02d94)). The table turns these failures into design rules.

| What went wrong elsewhere | Rule for your build |
|---|---|
| Recognisers limited to a fixed list stalled: Samsung sat at 33–37 fresh foods for years, and FridgeCam recognised nothing | Use a general-purpose cloud vision model behind one swappable function, and train nothing |
| Fixed cameras miss door bins, drawers, the freezer and back rows | A person with a phone is the main sensor; a fixed camera only supplements it |
| Rituals at the door: hold it up, wait for the beep, avoid door pockets | Never ask for per-item actions; take one quick scan per zone afterwards |
| Expiry dates typed by hand, and stale lists that need manual deletion ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/home/kitchen-tech/samsung-bespoke-fridge-with-ai-review-all-the-bells-and-whistles-140000099.html)) | No date entry: use the first-seen date plus typical shelf life, and let zones nobody has scanned recently fade to "not sure" |
| Detecting removals was the weakest link, even for Samsung | Treat a missing item cautiously: "not sure", then "probably gone", and "gone" only when someone confirms |
| A vendor shutdown bricked the hardware (HNGRYnsite) | Own the data and the device protocol, and treat the AI model as replaceable |
| "Freshness" gadgets were really timers: Ovie's tags ([New Atlas](https://newatlas.com/ovie-smarterware/52890/)), and DecayDock "cannot actually detect spoilage" ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2026/05/20/decaydock-keeps-track-of-spoilage/)) | Say "day 9 of a typical N, check it", never "fresh", "spoiled" or "safe" |

None of the products or apps surveyed says it compares one photo scan with the next to work out what was used up. No search targeted that feature, so it may exist somewhere unsurveyed. Even so, a phone-first household tool can beat a $3,400 fridge on three fronts. The first is that scan-to-scan comparison. The second is covering the whole house, including the freezer, door bins, drawers and pantry, which are exactly where commercial cameras are blind. The third is a ranked "use these first" list after each scan.

## A sub-cent vision call reads the shelf, and your code decides what changed

### Haiku 5.5 is the default bet, but your own photos pick the winner

The cheap tiers from all three major AI providers accept several photos per request and can be forced to return JSON that matches a schema (a fixed output format). That leaves price and accuracy on your own photos as the deciding factors. Claude Haiku 5.5 has the cheapest verified price: **US$0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens** for prompts up to 100,000 tokens. A longer request is billed entirely at $0.50/$2.50, and Anthropic's Batch API halves all its prices ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). GPT-5.4 nano ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-5-4-mini-and-nano/)) and Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite ([Google](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)) cost two to three times as much per token. The cost table further down lists all six candidates.

Claude charges for an image by its size, at ⌈width/28⌉ × ⌈height/28⌉ tokens. A photo resized to **1568 × 1176 costs 2,352 tokens, about US$0.00024 on Haiku 5.5**. An untouched 12-megapixel photo is scaled down to roughly 4,700 tokens, close to the 4,784-token cap of the high-resolution tier ([Claude vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)). The docs grant that tier to "Claude 4.7 and later models", so Haiku 5.5 presumably qualifies; confirm by counting the tokens on a real photo.

There is a real case for Gemini. A 2026 bioRxiv benchmark of 10 approaches on 3,229 food images rated **Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite best for ingredient recognition at the lowest cost** ([bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.26.740845v1)), and Samsung chose Gemini for its fridges. The case for Haiku is the cheapest verified price, strict structured outputs, and the Anthropic SDK code that Cut Protocol already contains. Model names changed repeatedly through 2026, and pricing aggregators disagree on whether GPT-5.4 has been replaced by "GPT-5.6" or "GPT-6" tiers ([CloudZero](https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/openai-pricing/); [BenchLM](https://benchlm.ai/openai/api-pricing)). So put the model call behind one function with a provider-neutral prompt and schema, and let a test decide.

To run that test, label 30–50 of your own fridge, freezer and pantry photos with what is really in them. Run Sonnet or Opus at low effort first to set a quality ceiling. Then run Haiku 5.5, Gemini Flash-Lite and GPT-5.4 nano, and keep the cheapest model that clears your bar on three numbers: how many real items it finds, how many of its items are real, and how accurately it counts items with five or fewer units. Whichever model wins, use a paid tier. Google's free-tier terms let human reviewers read API inputs ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Oct/17/gemini-terms-of-service/); [GeoToolbox](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/gemini-api-pricing.md)). Anthropic says images sent for vision are not used for training and are not stored beyond the request ([Claude vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)).

A few request rules prevent most errors. Claude "does not parse or receive any metadata from images", and it makes more mistakes on rotated or low-quality images. So **rotate the pixels themselves** to match the phone's orientation before upload, and strip GPS data while you are at it. Keep each request to 20 images or fewer; above that, a stricter 2,000-pixel size limit applies ([Claude vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)). Structured outputs (`output_config.format` with a JSON schema) guarantee valid JSON, within limits. Claude's schemas allow no numeric minimums or maximums and at most 24 optional and 16 union-typed parameters, and every object needs `additionalProperties: false`. A refusal or a `max_tokens` cut-off can still return JSON that doesn't match, and a field named "reasoning" can trigger a refusal ([Claude structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)). Haiku 5.5 thinks before answering by default, and those thinking tokens count against `max_tokens`. So set effort low, leave generous room, and check `stop_reason` before parsing the reply ([Claude models overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/overview)).

Running recognition on your own hardware doesn't pay off in Phase 1. In a 16-image PhotoPrism test, the open Qwen3-VL 4B and 8B models, run locally with Ollama, found 75% of expected labels with a default prompt. When the prompt asked for a specific number of labels, they found **97%** ([PhotoPrism](https://docs.photoprism.app/developer-guide/vision/model-comparison/)). That "count first" trick is worth borrowing in any prompt. A local model makes sense only if you already run a GPU machine around the clock. Custom-trained detectors (YOLOv8n and a modified YOLACT) scored 90–95% mAP (a standard detection-accuracy score) on fridge food, but only for 80 items photographed more than 100 times each ([Frontiers in AI](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2024.1442948/full)). An ESP32-CAM running its own Edge Impulse model scored 98.1% in training but **71–99% in testing, depending on where objects sat** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/389362447_Application_of_Edge_Impulse_and_ESP32-CAM_in_Smart_Freezer_Systems_for_Object_Recognition)). Use the microcontroller to take the picture, not to recognise what is in it.

### Models count and match poorly, so compare inventories, not pixels

No public benchmark tests current models on real household fridge photos, but the indirect evidence points one way. On counting partly hidden objects (the CAPTURe benchmark), the best model tested, GPT-4o, had a **14.75% error rate where humans were near-perfect**, and every model did worse once objects were occluded ([CAPTURe, ICCV 2025](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2025/papers/Pothiraj_CAPTURE_Evaluating_Spatial_Reasoning_in_Vision_Language_Models_via_Occluded_ICCV_2025_paper.pdf)). On judging whether two images show the same physical object, **no model exceeded 50% accuracy** ([MMVM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.04670)). No model listed every item correctly in five shopping-basket photos, though adding catalog reference images helped ([arXiv 2501.04150](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.04150)).

Spotting change works better. On consecutive images, Gemini reached 76.56% average precision, against 26.52% for a CLIP image-similarity baseline, and **a self-check prompt raised precision from 57.83% to 81.74%** ([Visual Chronicles](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08727v3)). These are mostly 2024–25 models, and current ones may do better.

The conclusion still holds. Compare structured inventory lists rather than images or embeddings, and skip trying to re-identify individual objects in v1. Treat counts above about five small items as approximate, as Claude's documentation warns ([Claude vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)). The cheapest accuracy gains are physical: one straight-on photo per shelf, door bins shot separately, and drawers pulled out. Label leftovers with masking tape, writing the contents and date, because models read text far more reliably than they tell identical containers apart. Samsung now markets its ability to read handwritten labels ([Ground News](https://ground.news/article/samsung-electronics-unveils-ai-refrigerator-combining-gemini-and-ai-even-reading-handwritten-labels)).

### One call per scan, then deterministic merge rules

A scan is one session of zone-tagged photos (fridge top shelf, door, left crisper, freezer drawer, pantry shelf 2), and it gets one model call. The request puts the photos first, each labelled "Image n: zone". After them come four inputs: the previous inventory for the zones being photographed, with stable IDs; the household's list of standard item names; any receipt lines since the last scan; and today's date. The fixed part of the prompt can be cached. It tells the model to report only what is visible and to work shelf by shelf, counting distinct products first. It should reuse names from the list and never invent a brand, and report an item once even if two photos overlap. It gives fill levels only for see-through containers, says "uncertain" rather than guessing, and asks at most three one-tap questions. The key design decision is which facts come from the model and which ones your code keeps:

| Returned by the model on every scan | Kept by your code, never by the model |
|---|---|
| Per photo: zone, a usable flag and any issues (dark, glare, blurry, frost, items hidden), so the app can ask for a retake instead of trusting an empty result | `first_seen` and `last_seen`, with the scans behind them |
| Per item: a standard name reused from your list, readable label text, category and container, count and whether it is exact, fill level, sealed/opened/unknown, high/medium/low confidence, which earlier item it matches and how (same item, extra unit, new, uncertain), and a short note on the evidence | Miss count and status (present, not sure, probably gone, gone, used, tossed) |
| Per previous item in a photographed zone: seen, not seen, zone not photographed, possibly moved, or uncertain | Shelf-life category, use-by window and the "day N" flag |
| Up to three one-tap questions | Anything a person edited: names, dates, brand, notes, opened date |

Category, container, fill level, opened state and confidence should each be limited to a fixed list of values (enums). Every field should be required, with "unknown" or an empty string as the fallback, which keeps the schema inside Claude's limits. Confidence is high/medium/low rather than a number because the schema can't bound numbers, and three levels are easier to set thresholds on.

Your code then applies the merge rules. A matched item gets a new `last_seen`, and its `first_seen` never changes. An unmatched observation becomes a new item, dated to the scan, or to the purchase date if a receipt line matches within about three days. An item missing from a zone that *was* photographed gains a miss. One miss makes it "not sure", since it may be behind the milk. Two misses in a row, or one on a full scan after shopping, make it "probably gone". Only a tap from a person makes it gone, used or tossed. Items in zones nobody photographed are left alone.

A before-and-after comparison can mistake a move for a removal plus an addition, so compare the whole session at once. An item that vanishes from one zone while the same name appears in another counts as moved and keeps its date. A re-buy after "gone" starts a new lot with a fresh clock. Items present on the very first scan show as "day ≥ N", because their true age is unknown.

When the model leaves a match uncertain, your code should try an exact name match first. If that fails, it falls back to a fuzzy match (a token-set score of about 85 using a library such as rapidfuzz, plus the same category and container), and finally asks the user. Cap the confirmation queue at three to five cards per scan, each with a cropped thumbnail. Claude's bounding boxes are approximate ([Claude vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)), which is fine for a crop. If your test set shows false removals, add a second call that asks the model to re-check each claimed removal, based on the self-check result above; it roughly doubles cost. Treat every threshold here as a starting point, since no published data covers miss counts or how many confirmation cards people tolerate.

Receipts help but should stay optional. A model reading the receipt image directly scored **87.46% versus 47.00%** for a pipeline that ran OCR first ([arXiv 2509.04469](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.04469)). A per-store abbreviation table built from your corrections handles cryptic item names ([tempo PR #66](https://github.com/nicoladebbia/tempo/pull/66)). No source documented how Superstore, Save-On-Foods, Sobeys/Safeway or Co-op receipts abbreviate items, though Costco's app shows in-warehouse receipts ([Costco.ca](https://www.costco.ca/costco-app.html)). A matched receipt line supplies an exact purchase date for `first_seen` and a naming hint for the next scan. It is also a way to log freezer and pantry items that never get photographed.

## First-seen dates and FoodKeeper turn photos into freshness flags

### FoodKeeper covers the long tail, and Health Canada overrides the risky foods

USDA's FoodKeeper is the only free, machine-readable government shelf-life dataset the research found. The USDA's food safety agency publishes it on data.gov as XLS and JSON in English, Spanish and Portuguese. Its listed last update, **22 January 2025**, had not changed at a September 2026 check of the catalog ([data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/fsis-foodkeeper-data)). USDA's latest stated count is **more than 650 foods and beverages** ([USDA](https://www.usda.gov/about-usda/news/blog/usda-updates-foodkeeper-app-include-new-food-items)), and a mirror lists the data as CC0, meaning public domain ([AmeriGEOSS](https://data.amerigeoss.org/dataset/fsis-foodkeeper-data1)). Each product carries minimum, maximum and unit fields for pantry, fridge and freezer. Separate variants cover time from purchase (the `DOP_` fields) and time after opening. Some units aren't numbers at all, such as "When Ripe", "Indefinitely" and "Not Recommended" ([FSIS data dictionary](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media_file/2020-06/Data-Documentation-FoodKeeper-Application.pdf)).

The download links are plain HTTP, and no maintained npm or PyPI wrapper exists. So download the file once, reshape it into one row per product, location and timing basis, and commit a dated copy to your repo. Use the `DOP_` fields as the default clock. DOP most plausibly means "date of purchase", and the date an item is first seen approximates its purchase date. The file's JSON layout and its actual spinach window went unverified. Whether "day 9" triggers "check it" depends on numbers you will read from the file. USDA itself calls the times "useful guidelines and … not hard-and-fast rules" ([USDA](https://www.usda.gov/about-usda/news/blog/usda-updates-foodkeeper-app-include-new-food-items)).

For high-risk foods, use Health Canada's numbers instead. Health Canada says its fridge times are for safety and its freezer times for quality. It recommends 4 °C or colder in the fridge and −18 °C in the freezer ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/general-food-safety-tips/safe-food-storage.html)). A 2023 copy of its chart gives these fridge times: beef 2–4 days, ground meat 1–2 days, poultry 2–3 days, fish 3–4 days and shell eggs 3–4 weeks ([Health Canada chart, 2023 copy](https://www.slfnha.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Safe-food-storage-Canada.ca_.pdf)). Its leftovers guide gives cooked dishes 3–4 days in the fridge and 2–3 months frozen ([Canada.ca](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/health/publications/food-nutrition/infographic-leftovers.html)).

Canadian and US guidance disagree in places. Health Canada allows raw poultry 2–3 days where US guidance says 1–2, and gives beef steaks 2–4 days against the US 3–5 ([Minnesota Department of Health](https://www.health.mn.gov/people/foodsafety/store/cold.html); [FoodSafety.gov](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts)). Pick one rule and write it down; the shorter value is the cautious trigger for "use soon". No downloadable Canadian dataset exists, so expect to type in about a dozen Health Canada rows by hand.

### Canadian labels give perishables a machine-readable anchor

Canadian labelling rules help. Prepackaged foods that keep 90 days or less must carry a best-before date, though fresh produce is exempt ([CFIA](https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/consumers/date-labels-food)). When a year is shown, it comes first, followed by a bilingual two-letter month code: JA, FE, MR, AL, MA, JN, JL, AU, SE, OC, NO or DE ([Food and Drug Regulations B.01.007](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.01.007.html)). Your date parser must therefore read "26 OC 20" as **20 October 2026, not 26 October 2020**. It also has to handle dates with no year and US-format dates on imported products.

A readable best-before date is a better anchor than first-seen, but only while the package is sealed. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) says a best-before date "is not an indicator of food safety, neither before nor after the date" ([CFIA industry guide](https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/date-markings-and-storage-instructions)). It also warns that "some foods will become unsafe before they spoil" ([CFIA](https://inspection.canada.ca/en/inspect-and-protect/food-safety/three-facts-about-best-dates-can-save)).

Rank date sources from strongest to weakest. An expiration date wins, but it is rare. Next comes a printed best-before on a sealed package. Then a "packaged on" date plus its stated shelf life, as on store-packed meat; that date is earlier than your first sighting. Then a date someone typed in. Last comes first-seen plus the FoodKeeper or Health Canada window.

When scans are far apart, show an item's age as a range. Say an item was first photographed nine days ago, and that shelf's previous scan was twelve days ago. It could have arrived any time in between, so it shows as "day 9–12". Phase 2's frequent photos shrink that gap to hours.

### Classify into a closed list, and never let the model invent a shelf life

Matching "baby spinach clamshell" to the right shelf-life row is where naive designs go wrong. In tests of linking food names to a large food database, general-purpose models given a few examples scored at most about **0.33 macro-F1** (an accuracy score where 1.0 is perfect), against 0.93–0.98 for a model fine-tuned for the task ([FoodyLLM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12927182/)). So turn it into a choice from a closed list. Clean up the name, then shortlist candidate rows by keyword; APIs built on FoodKeeper expose search keywords for each product ([jelera/food-shelflife-db](https://github.com/jelera/food-shelflife-db)). Let the model pick a row ID from the shortlist or answer UNKNOWN, and reject any ID that isn't on the list. Save every confirmed mapping per household, so the same phrase never needs a model call again.

Fixed rules override the classifier. Anything labelled as leftovers, or kept in an opaque reusable container, maps to cooked leftovers. Raw meat, poultry and fish map to Health Canada rows, and anything in the freezer uses freezer times. Unknown items get an "Unclassified, tap to label" card and a cautious placeholder such as "check in 3 days", shown as your own choice rather than official guidance.

Open Food Facts helps identify barcoded products, and Cut Protocol already calls its barcode lookup ([foods.js](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/backend/src/routes/foods.js)). It holds no storage times, though, and its expiration-date field is free text copied from a single photographed package ([Open Food Facts wiki](https://wiki.openfoodfacts.org/Data_fields)).

### Word freshness as a reminder, never a safety verdict

Three states keep the wording honest. Green reads "within typical storage time" and never says "safe". Yellow reads "use soon / check it" and starts at the low end of the storage window. Red depends on the kind of limit. For quality-limited foods like produce and condiments, it reads "past typical storage time, check before using". For leftovers, raw meat and fish, and deli meats, it reads "past Health Canada's recommended fridge time". The app should never suggest a sniff test, because disease-causing bacteria generally don't change how food looks, smells or tastes ([Saudi Food and Drug Authority](https://sfda.gov.sa/en/faq/do-spoilage-bacteria-make-people-sick)).

Show the basis for each flag, such as "first seen 9 days ago (±2) · leafy greens: FoodKeeper N–M days". Let visual signs such as wilting or mould shorten an estimate but never lengthen it. When the model spots mould, show USDA's rule: on hard cheese, cut at least 2.5 cm around the mould, and throw soft foods away ([Mayo Clinic](https://mayoclinic.com/health/food-and-nutrition/AN01024)). A photo rarely shows whether something has been opened, so either treat items like milk as opened from their first sighting or ask once. Moving an item into the freezer stops its fridge clock and starts a quality clock. Moving it back starts a short "thawed" window. That is Grocy's approach, but keep the original dates ([Grocy docs](https://github.com/grocy/grocy-docs/blob/master/tutorials/food.md)).

Every one of these windows assumes a fridge at 4 °C or colder, and a 361-fridge Scottish study found an **average of 5.17 °C, with 55.4% of fridges averaging above 5 °C** ([Food Standards Scotland](https://www.foodstandards.gov.scot/sites/default/files/migration/downloads/FROST_Final_Report_June_2025.pdf)). That makes a cheap temperature sensor the most useful thing Phase 2 can add.

## A separate household PWA costs a few dollars a year to run

### Keep it out of Cut Protocol for now, but borrow its best parts

Cut Protocol is a React 19, Vite 8 and Tailwind 4 single-page app with an Express 5 and Prisma 6 backend. It runs SQLite in a Windows desktop build (Electron), and uses Supabase's Postgres database and login service when hosted on Railway ([frontend/package.json](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/frontend/package.json); [backend/package.json](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/backend/package.json)). Its Anthropic calls run on the server, optionally through a relay that "holds the only copy" of the API key. The relay accepts a separate token for each device and caps spending at **US$2 a day, $20 a month and $0.50 a request** ([relay/src/config.js](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/relay/src/config.js)). It is built on the principle that anything enforced on the client "is therefore advisory" ([relay/src/server.js](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/relay/src/server.js)). Several other pieces are worth reusing: the `LlmUsage` cost table, the 14,122-food library linked to USDA records and UPC barcodes, the Open Food Facts barcode lookup, and the grocery-list code that sorts items into store sections ([README](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/README.md); [groceryList.js](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/backend/src/lib/groceryList.js)).

But on 24 August 2026 you cut Cut Protocol back to "One user. One surface. No login, no paywall." and deferred the AI layer ([CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/CLAUDE.md); [HANDOFF-CURRENT.md](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/HANDOFF-CURRENT.md)). The fridge tracker needs exactly what you set aside: a hosted backend you can reach from a grocery store, logins for each household member, AI calls and the camera. The current code works against all four. Single-user mode refuses to start on a hosted server ([singleUser.js](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/backend/src/lib/singleUser.js)). Every response sends `Permissions-Policy: camera=()`, which blocks camera access ([backend/server.js](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/backend/server.js)). The database is per-user, with no concept of a household. And Supabase's connection pooler caps out at 15 connections, a limit that has already deadlocked a deploy ([docker-entrypoint.sh](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/docker-entrypoint.sh)).

So build a separate small app that copies Cut Protocol's conventions rather than its code. A small repo is easier for AI coding tools to keep in view, and a bug in the fridge app can't break the meal planner. Connect the two later through a read-only inventory summary. Cut Protocol's grocery list can then mark lines "probably have" instead of silently deleting them, which fits its rule that "every automatic adjustment is logged, visible, and reversible" ([CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/CLAUDE.md)). If you send vision calls through the relay, its 256 KB request-size limit will reject a photo sent inline as base64 text. Raise `RELAY_MAX_BODY_BYTES`, or pass images as short-lived signed links instead.

### A PWA that hands off to the phone's own camera beats a native app

A PWA needs no app-store fees and no Mac, and one codebase serves both iPhone and Android. Since Safari 26, any site added to the iPhone Home Screen opens as a web app by default ([WebKit](https://webkit.org/blog/17333/webkit-features-in-safari-26-0/)). Installing is a one-time Share → Add to Home Screen, because iOS has no install prompt, and push notifications work only after that install ([Mobiloud](https://www.mobiloud.com/blog/progressive-web-apps-ios/)).

The known iOS weak spot is the live camera view inside the browser. Home Screen web apps have long been reported to lose camera permission, or ask for it again, when the user moves between pages ([WebKit bug 215884](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=215884); [Scandit](https://support.scandit.com/hc/en-us/articles/360008443011-Why-does-iOS-keep-asking-for-camera-permissions)). The workaround is a plain file input with `capture="environment"`. It opens the phone's own camera app at full resolution, with its autofocus and HDR, and hands back a file. That advice rests on platform knowledge the research couldn't re-check, so test it on every phone in the house in week one. Resize each photo on the phone to about 1,568 pixels on the long edge and save it as JPEG at roughly 0.8 quality. Re-drawing the photo on a canvas to do that should also strip its EXIF metadata, but confirm that the GPS location is really gone.

Native Swift is out without a Mac. Expo or React Native earns its place only if reliable local notifications or a native camera become must-haves. Cut Protocol's roadmap already prefers wrapping the web app with Capacitor, "roughly a day of setup", over a "multi-week rewrite" ([roadmap/08-mobile-packaging.md](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/roadmap/08-mobile-packaging.md)).

Distribution then costs nothing for a PWA. Installing on iPhones through TestFlight needs the **US$99-a-year Apple Developer Program (about C$139)** ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/support/enrollment/)). Google Play costs US$25 once, but new personal accounts must first run a **closed test with 12 testers for 14 days** ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14151465)), which is awkward for a family of three. Installing an Android app directly from a file (sideloading) still works. Google's developer verification has a hobbyist tier for up to 20 devices without government ID. Its 2026 registration requirement covers only Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand before going global in 2027 ([Android Developers Blog](https://developer.android.com/blog/posts/android-developer-verification-rolling-out-to-all-developers-on-play-console-and-android-developer-console)). Your household's mix of phones decides which of these routes matters.

### Supabase gives you sign-in, private storage and a key-holding function for free

A new Supabase project, your second free one, fits best because you already know its Postgres database and Google sign-in. The free plan gives **two active projects, 500 MB of database, 1 GB of file storage and 5 GB of egress (outbound data transfer)**. But it **pauses a project after a week without database activity**, and sources disagree on whether it includes backups ([UI Bakery](https://uibakery.io/blog/supabase-pricing); [MakerKit](https://makerkit.dev/blog/saas/supabase-pricing)). Your own Cut Protocol notes already propose a free UptimeRobot check every 5 minutes to keep a project awake ([V2-DELTA.md](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/V2-DELTA.md)). If you use Google sign-in, add each household member as a test user while the Google OAuth app is in Testing mode, as Cut Protocol's still is ([TONIGHT-RUNBOOK.md](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/docs/deploy/TONIGHT-RUNBOOK.md)). Email magic links are the alternative.

Unlike in Cut Protocol, turn the Data API on, so the PWA can read and write through `supabase-js` under database rules (row-level security) that limit each household to its own data. Keep photos in a private bucket. Put the vision key in exactly one Edge Function, `/scan` ([RWIT](https://www.rwit.io/blog/supabase-edge-functions-ai-microservices/)). It verifies the user and checks the household's usage caps in a Postgres counter table, because Supabase's built-in limits apply per project, not per user. It then loads the photos on the server, calls the model and writes structured rows.

Each alternative has a catch. Cloudflare Workers with R2 storage and the D1 database never pauses and charges nothing for egress, but you build sign-in yourself. Since 1 September 2026, D1's free tier also rejects queries once its daily limits are used up ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/changelog/post/2026-09-01-d1-free-tier-limit-enforcement/)). PocketBase runs as one program on a roughly US$4 server, but it hasn't reached version 1.0 and backups are your job ([PocketBase FAQ](https://pocketbase.io/faq/)). Firebase Storage has required the pay-as-you-go Blaze plan since February 2026 ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/androve2k/spark-vs-blaze-the-firebase-pricing-guide-i-wish-id-read-sooner-onb)). A small Express service on Railway's Hobby plan (US$5 a month, including $5 of usage) would let you reuse Cut Protocol's Node code ([Railway](https://docs.railway.app/reference/pricing)). Grocy could serve as the database, but it is built around recording every purchase and every use by hand. That is the "warehouse logistics" that drove the Galaxus writer away, and your design's whole point is to infer those events from photos instead.

Neither the phone nor the future ESP32 should ever hold the AI key or Supabase's service key. Give each camera its own revocable token, stored as a hash and limited to uploading for one household and location. Cap each household at roughly 20–30 scans a day and 4–6 photos per scan, with daily and monthly dollar limits like the relay's. Reject duplicate uploads by comparing file hashes.

Anthropic's vision docs call images "ephemeral and not stored beyond the duration of the API request" ([Claude vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)). Its privacy center describes deleting API data within 30 days, while a third-party blog says seven ([Anthropic Privacy Center](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/7996866-how-long-do-you-store-my-organization-s-data); [Char](https://char.com/blog/anthropic-data-retention-policy)), so plan on up to 30. Delete your own stored photos automatically after 30–90 days and keep only the extracted rows. At about 300 KB per resized photo, 15 photos a week come to roughly 230 MB a year. Storage will never be the constraint; free-tier pausing and backups will.

### Thirteen tables and a ten-second habit

Model the data in three layers: an append-only record of what each scan saw, a current inventory worked out from that record, and a log of every correction.

| Tables | What they hold | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| `households`, `household_members` | The sharing unit (timezone America/Edmonton) and who belongs | Every row carries `household_id`, and row-level security checks membership |
| `locations` | Fridge, freezer, pantry and their zones (door, crisper, shelf 2), each tagged with a storage type | The storage type picks which FoodKeeper column applies |
| `scans`, `photos` | One scan per session from a phone or a device, with model, prompt version, tokens, cost and status; photos with a hash and a delete-after date | `source = device` lets Phase 2 reuse everything |
| `sightings` | What the model reported for each photo, kept small and forever | You can re-run matching with better rules later |
| `items` | Each inventory lot: `first_seen`, `last_seen`, `missed_scans`, status, opened date, any date a person set, and an age-unknown flag | A re-buy after "gone" is a new row with a new clock |
| `item_types`, `shelf_life_categories` | Standard names with aliases, linked to FoodKeeper and Health Canada rows and, optionally, Cut Protocol food IDs | Stops "baby spinach" and "spinach" drifting apart |
| `corrections` | Every confirm, rename, merge, mark-gone, move and date fix | Teaches aliases and suppresses repeat mistakes |
| `devices`, `shopping_items`, `usage_ledger` | Camera tokens, shopping-list lines, and tokens and cost per scan | The ledger enforces spending caps, as `LlmUsage` does in Cut Protocol |

The scan has to be quicker than the point where people give up. Open the app to one big Scan button, with Fridge, Freezer and Pantry chips that remember the last choice. Take one to four shots per zone with the phone's camera, and let the upload finish after the phone goes back in your pocket. Aim for under 15 seconds per zone, done while putting groceries away. Forky observes that "most people do a full re-scan after a grocery run" ([Forky](https://forkyai.com/fridge-scanner/)).

The result is one card: New (+5), Gone? (−3), and Still here (12, collapsed). It has a big "Looks right" button, one-tap fixes, and Used it / Tossed it / Still there on each Gone? row. Changes nobody reviews are accepted after 12–24 hours but stay marked as lower-confidence. End every scan with a ranked "use these first" list, the immediate payoff that inventory apps usually lack.

In the store, group items by store section (reuse Cut Protocol's classifier) into three buckets. Probably have means seen in a recent scan. Not sure means missed once, last scanned too long ago, or past typical shelf life. Probably out covers the rest. Show each location's last-scan age ("Fridge scanned 2 days ago") and freshness chips like "spinach · day 9 · check it". Keep a copy of the last snapshot on the phone for dead zones in the store. When a location goes 10 days without a scan, let its items drift to Not sure rather than nagging.

Notifications should be one opt-in digest ("3 things to use soon"), matching Cut Protocol's "no notification spam" rule ([CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/CLAUDE.md)). Probably-out items become one-tap shopping-list suggestions, never silent additions or orders. Samsung holds the same line even with Instacart built in ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/106204-samsung-ai-fridges-soon-order-groceries-you-instacart.html)).

| Setup (excluding AI calls) | Cost | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| PWA on free static hosting + Supabase Free | C$0/month | Needs a keep-alive ping; backups uncertain |
| Cloudflare Workers + R2 + D1 | C$0/month | Never pauses; you build sign-in |
| PWA + Express on Railway Hobby + Supabase Free | ~C$7/month | Reuses Cut Protocol's Node and Anthropic code |
| PocketBase on a small rented server | ~C$6–14/month | You own backups, HTTPS certificates and upgrades |
| Add Supabase Pro | +~C$35/month | No pausing, plus backups; overkill for one family |
| iPhone app via TestFlight | ~C$139/year | Only if you move beyond the PWA |
| Google Play listing | ~C$35 once | Plus the 12-tester, 14-day closed test |

## The fridge camera should be a USB-powered supplement, not the source of truth

### No single camera sees a fridge

Geometry limits a fixed camera. With the door closed, a door-mounted lens sits only 10–15 cm from the shelf fronts. Seeing the front edge of a shelf 50 cm above or below it takes roughly **150° of vertical view**. That is why FridgeCam used a fisheye lens, and reviewers still said it "can't see everywhere" ([Expert Reviews](https://www.expertreviews.co.uk/gadgets/1407382/smarter-fridgecam-review)). The Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 Wide (120° diagonal, 102° × 67°) covers about 124 × 66 cm of a back wall 50 cm away ([Raspberry Pi](https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/camera/sensor-assembly-product-brief.pdf)). That is roughly one shelf's width and two or three shelves' height. Full coverage takes one camera per shelf zone. Closed crisper drawers can't be seen at all, and a top-down view of the door sees only the top bin. Samsung covers none of these either ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/home/kitchen-tech/samsung-bespoke-fridge-with-ai-review-all-the-bells-and-whistles-140000099.html)).

The camera's realistic job is to take frequent "what changed on the main shelves" snapshots and give you a photo to check from the store. Phone scans keep covering door bins, drawers, the freezer and the pantry. A second camera is best spent on a stacked upper-and-lower view of the main compartment, or on the freezer with a heated window. Mount it rigidly, because consistent framing matters more than coverage when comparing scans. These coverage figures are calculated from lens specs and assumed fridge dimensions, so measure your own fridge.

### Shoot after the door closes, with your own light

Watching items cross the doorway is what forced Samsung's one-at-a-time ritual. Vending-machine research also shows recognition accuracy drops while items are moving ([Towards New Retail](https://dl2link.com/Selected%20Journal%20Publications/Towards%20New%20Retail%20A%20Benchmark%20Dataset%20for%20Smart%20Unmanned%20Vending%20Machines.pdf)). Trigger instead on a magnetic reed switch, which also works in a freezer with no light. fridge-core reads one on pin GPIO13, though it takes its photo when the door opens ([Home Assistant Community](https://community.home-assistant.io/t/fridge-cam-ai-content-recognition-esp32-cam-ha-blueprint-a-custom-lovelace-card/1023750)). A light sensor that sees the fridge lamp come on is the no-wiring alternative, used in m0nklabs' firmware ([m0nklabs](https://github.com/m0nklabs/fridge-cam-firmware)).

Wait until the door has stayed shut; start with a delay of 30–120 seconds that resets if the door reopens. Then switch on diffuse white LEDs behind a frosted cover and take a burst of three frames. Keep the sharpest, judged by Laplacian variance, the same sharpness score that rejects a fogged frame. Door-closed shots are steady, repeatable and free of hands and people.

On the server, skip frames whose perceptual hash (a fingerprint of the image) hasn't changed, and process at most one scan per location every few minutes. Device photos become ordinary scans with `source='device'`, and they update only the zones the camera sees. Fifteen captures a day work out to roughly US$0.65 a month on Haiku 5.5, or about US$13 on Sonnet 5.5, before unchanged frames are skipped. That estimate comes from [Anthropic's prices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing).

### Pick an ESP32-S3 with a 5 MP sensor, or a Pi if labels matter

M5Stack's Unit CamS3-5MP pairs a 5 MP sensor with an 88° lens fixed-focused at 0.6 m, about the distance from the door to the back wall ([M5Stack](https://docs.m5stack.com/en/unit/Unit_CamS3-5MP)).

Dubovsky's 640×480 frames are probably too coarse to read labels, and reviewers dismissed FridgeCam as "essentially a low-resolution camera" ([Expert Reviews](https://www.expertreviews.co.uk/home-garden/large-appliances/smarter-fridgecam-review)). For tools, budget a fine-tip temperature-controlled soldering iron, plus tweezers and magnification for the ESP32-CAM antenna fix. You'll also want a multimeter with a microamp range and a USB power meter to check sleep current.

### Run a USB cable through the gasket before you think about batteries

Battery power is where ESP32 camera projects quietly fail. The ESP32-CAM draws about 180 mA at 5 V, 310 mA with its flash on, and a nominal 6 mA in deep sleep ([Joy-it datasheet](https://www.lextronic.fr/lextronic_doc/joy-it/SBC-ESP32-CAM.pdf)). Elektor traced its sleep drain to the voltage regulator and camera, and reached about 0.5 mA only with a redesigned supply ([Elektor](https://www.elektormagazine.de/labs/esp32-low-consumption-power-supply-for-lithium-battery-solar-recharge)). Owners of the XIAO S3 Sense measure 2–2.8 mA asleep with the camera attached and about 90 mA after a capture, because software can't switch off the camera's power ([Seeed forum](https://forum.seeedstudio.com/t/xiao-esp32s3-sense-camera-sleep-current/271258)). One OV5640 board sat near 100 mA while asleep ([esp32.com](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=126832)), enough to drain an 18650 cell in about a day. By rough estimate, a stock ESP32-CAM lasts 2–5 weeks per 18650 at 30 captures a day. Only hard power-gating stretches that to months: the door switch turns on a load switch, and the microcontroller releases it after uploading.

Cold charging settles the question. Lithium-ion cells should be fast-charged only between 5 °C and 45 °C, at reduced current below 5 °C, and **never at or below freezing** ([Battery University-derived guide](https://www.elfa.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Charging-at-high-and-low-temperatures.pdf)). A 3 °C fridge is already too cold for the simple chargers on hobby boards. For v1, run a thin flat USB cable under the door gasket; a 1.3 mm probe cable is sold to fit the roughly 1.5 mm gap ([MOCREO](https://mocreo.com/product/connection-cables/)). Keep the certified 5 V adapter outside the fridge. Check the seal by closing the door on a slip of paper and pulling it, and watch for frost near the cable. Don't drill the cabinet: refrigerant lines run in the walls, which is why Home Assistant users who do drill stay close to the door edge ([Home Assistant Community](https://community.home-assistant.io/t/fridge-freezer-temp-sensors/744045)). If a freezer camera ever needs batteries, use lithium AA cells rated to −40 °C ([Energizer](https://data.energizer.com/pdfs/liaa_sellsheet_may2010.pdf)).

### Wi-Fi leaks out, but fog and frost need a plan

The steel box is a leaky Faraday cage. The magnetic door gasket is a non-metal gap metres long that 2.4 GHz signals pass through, and field reports say devices inside usually connect: "you would think it was a Faraday cage, but it does indeed get out" ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2023/07/27/esp32-freezer-alarm-keeps-tabs-on-tricky-door/)). Log signal strength (RSSI) with the door shut, because ESP32 boards become unstable below about −80 dBm ([espboards.dev](https://www.espboards.dev/troubleshooting/issues/wifi/esp32-wifi-signal-weak/)). If the signal is weak, start with an external antenna near the gasket. On the ESP32-CAM that means moving a tiny 0 Ω resistor ([Random Nerd Tutorials](https://randomnerdtutorials.com/esp32-cam-connect-external-antenna/)). On the XIAO, lowering transmit power to 8.5 dB cured its drop-outs ([DigiKey](https://www.digikey.ca/en/maker/projects/diy-ip-camera-with-the-seeed-xiao-esp32-sense-esphome-home-assistant/a015ed69dda64f0eafbdf12b664eea6d)). As a last resort, relay over ESP-NOW, Espressif's direct board-to-board radio link, to a USB-powered board beside the fridge.

Lens fog is the harder problem, and none of the DIY builds has reported how they handled it. Fog forms when the room air's dew point is warmer than the lens, and Haier has patented an anti-fog camera module for refrigerators ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11122203)). A dew-point calculation shows the seasonal swing. Room air at 21 °C and 40% humidity has a dew point near 6.9 °C, so it will fog a 3 °C lens. Dry, heated Alberta winter air, at an assumed 20% humidity, has a dew point near −2.8 °C and mostly won't. So expect summer to be the real test, and expect a −18 °C freezer lens to frost at nearly every opening.

The cheapest fix is to delay the shot and retry foggy frames. Next, recess the lens behind a small window and seal the electronics with desiccant. On USB power, you can also warm the window with about 0.25 W. For any freezer camera, treat USB power plus a heated window as mandatory. Print enclosures in PETG; both PLA and PETG turn brittle around −20 °C ([All3DP](https://all3dp.com/2/petg-vs-pla-3d-printing/)). Keep printed parts away from unwrapped food, and keep mains voltage outside the fridge entirely.

### Firmware should POST JPEGs straight into the same pipeline

Base the firmware on m0nklabs' ESP32-S3 + OV5640 build. It wakes, waits, lights the shelves, takes a burst, keeps the sharpest frame, uploads and goes back to sleep, with all AI on the server ([m0nklabs](https://github.com/m0nklabs/fridge-cam-firmware)). Write it in PlatformIO with the Arduino-ESP32 framework, which has the most examples for an AI coding tool to draw on. Send the raw JPEG in an HTTP POST with `Content-Type: image/jpeg`. Put device ID, zone, capture time, battery, RSSI and sharpness in headers, which sidesteps multipart-encoding bugs. Authenticate with the device token, save photos to the SD card when Wi-Fi fails, and enable over-the-air updates so the unit never has to come out.

Keep MQTT for small status messages only, since images overflow typical MQTT buffers ([Home Assistant Community](https://community.home-assistant.io/t/handling-large-mqtt-payloads/708996)). ESPHome is the fast path only if Home Assistant is your hub. Version 2026.5.3 broke OV2640 and OV3660 camera boards ([ESPHome #16926](https://github.com/esphome/esphome/issues/16926)), so pin a version that works. Home Assistant's `ai_task.generate_data` action and the LLM Vision integration can already call Anthropic, OpenAI, Gemini or Ollama ([Home Assistant](https://www.home-assistant.io/actions/ai_task.generate_data/); [LLM Vision](https://llm-vision.gitbook.io/getting-started/setup/providers)), but they would duplicate your own pipeline.

Add a temperature and humidity sensor before anything clever, because it directly tests the 4 °C assumption behind every storage window. Gas sensors are an experiment at best. In a meat study using Bosch's BME688 gas sensor, Bosch's own AI-Studio classifier was not very successful at identifying spoiled meat ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391822232_A_New_Approach_for_Machine_Learning-Based_Recognition_of_Meat_Species_Using_a_BME688_Gas_Sensors_Matrix)). MQ-series gas sensors are specified at 20 °C and 55% humidity, far from fridge conditions ([Winsen MQ-137 datasheet](https://www.winsen-sensor.com/d/files/semiconductor/mq137.pdf)). A "no odour" reading must never move an item back to green.

## Build in nine steps: measure first, solder last

The order front-loads the two questions no source could answer for you: how well a model reads *your* fridge, and whether your household keeps scanning. Hardware waits until the software has proven it's worth feeding automatically. Each step is a small, testable change of the kind AI coding tools handle well.

| Step | Build | Done when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Test set and model comparison: 30–50 labelled photos of fridge, freezer, door and pantry, and a script running Haiku 5.5, Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.4 nano and a Sonnet or Opus ceiling against the schema | Each model has scores for items found, false items and small counts, and you've picked a default, for a few dollars of API credit |
| 2 | Shelf-life table: FoodKeeper downloaded, reshaped and committed; a dozen Health Canada rows; your poultry and leftovers rule written down | Every item in the test set maps to a row or to UNKNOWN, and you know the real spinach window |
| 3 | PWA skeleton on a new Supabase project: household sign-in, row-level security, private bucket, manifest, file-input camera, resizing and rotation on the phone | Every phone in the house installs it and uploads a photo |
| 4 | The `/scan` function: photos and the previous inventory go in, schema JSON comes out and is saved as sightings, with a usage ledger and caps | A scan writes rows and logs its cost |
| 5 | The diff engine, review card and corrections log | Re-scanning an untouched fridge shows almost no changes, a removed item shows as "Gone?", and a moved jar keeps its date |
| 6 | Freshness flags, "use first" list, store view with offline copy, shopping list | "Do we have eggs?" gets answered with no signal in the store |
| 7 | A month of real use: tune miss thresholds, prompt and auto-accept timing; add optional receipts and a weekly digest | Scans stay under a minute and the household still does them |
| 8 | Phase 2 bench prototype: XIAO ESP32S3 + OV5640 (or an ESP32-CAM for a weekend build), USB power, reed switch, LEDs, a burst after the door closes, upload with a device token | Signal strength, fog-clearing time and coverage measured inside your own fridge |
| 9 | Mount and integrate: device scans that skip unchanged frames, temperature and humidity logging, a read-only link to Cut Protocol once its hosted features return | Fewer manual scans are needed, and "day 9–12" ranges shrink to "day 9" |

Added up, almost all the money goes on hardware.

| Cost line | Recommended path | Range |
|---|---|---|
| AI calls, phone scans | ~C$3–9/year (Haiku 5.5) | up to ~C$176/year (Sonnet 5.5, heavy use) |
| Hosting | C$0 (Supabase Free + static hosting) | ~C$84/year on Railway; ~C$420/year more for Supabase Pro |
| App distribution | C$0 (PWA) | ~C$139/year for TestFlight; ~C$35 once for Google Play |
| Phase 2 camera, one-time | ~C$85–165 (XIAO + OV5640) | ~C$80–185 per camera; ~C$150–370 for two |
| Phase 2 AI calls | ~C$11/year (Haiku 5.5) | ~C$218/year (Sonnet 5.5) |

That puts a realistic first year at roughly **C$100–185 before tax and tools**, close to nine-tenths of it the camera. Phase 1 on its own costs a few dollars a year.

## Conclusion

Recognising food was the hard problem a decade ago. Now it costs a fraction of a cent per photo and improves every time a provider ships a model. What is scarce is household attention and an inventory people can trust. So what you are really building is the logic that reconciles each scan with the last, wrapped around a ten-second habit. That logic keeps each item's identity stable, treats a missing item cautiously, protects human edits from the AI and keeps an honest clock. That makes the labelled photo set from step 1 the project's most durable asset. It turns every model release into a cheap re-test instead of a rewrite. It is also the only way to tune the miss thresholds and confirmation limits that no published study has measured.

Judge Phase 2 by a different yardstick. A fixed camera will never see the drawers and door bins your phone can, but it does two things a person won't. It photographs the main shelves many times a day, narrowing "day 9–12" to "day 9". And with a temperature sensor beside it, it tests the 4 °C assumption behind every storage window, which more than half the fridges in the Scottish study failed. If the camera tightens those dates and cuts the number of manual scans, it has earned its place. Once Cut Protocol's hosted, multi-user pieces return, the tracker's record of what is actually in the fridge is exactly the evidence Cut Protocol's own constitution says should win: "The user's observed data beats the model's prediction" ([CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/AlbertanCoder/cut-protocol/blob/9e1877ba71094236c3aedf4fa7ff9649e2bde233/CLAUDE.md)).
