Chapter 1 · The ballot

What voters are asked, and the words used on this page

Question 10 is a choice between two statements, not a yes-or-no question.[2] Cabinet set its wording on May 28, 2026, under the Referendum Act, and ordered that the result “is not to be binding.”[1] Premier Danielle Smith has said she treats 50 per cent plus one as a majority on this question.[3]

This page uses the following terms the same way for both options.

Remain
Option 1 on the ballot: “Alberta should remain a province of Canada.”
Start the process
Option 2 on the ballot: “The Government of Alberta should commence the legal process required under the Canadian Constitution to hold a binding provincial referendum on whether or not Alberta should separate from Canada.”
Separation
Alberta ceasing to be a province of Canada. The ballot uses the word “separate.”
Decided voters
Poll respondents who chose one of the two options. People who were unsure or would not say are left out, and the two options are scaled to add to 100%.
Poll average
A weighted average of polls on the ballot question. Only each firm’s latest poll is used, and newer and larger polls count more. The method chapter shows the formula.
Likely range
The range the result falls in about 8 times out of 10 if this page’s assumptions hold. It is based on how much polls disagree and how far final polls missed in five past referendums.

Ten questions, one about separation

Albertans answer ten questions on ten colour-coded ballots. Question 10, the separation question, is the first ballot in the stack.[4] Questions 1 to 5 are non-binding. Questions 6 to 9 ask the government to pursue constitutional changes with other provinces.[5]

  1. Questions 1 to 5 · Immigration and voting. Provincial control over immigration, who qualifies for provincially funded programs, a 12-month residency rule, fees for people with non-permanent status, and proof of citizenship to vote. Yes or no. Not binding.[6]
  2. Questions 6 to 9 · Constitutional changes. Proposals for the government to pursue with other provinces: provinces choosing superior court judges, abolishing the Senate, opting out of federal programs with compensation, and provincial laws taking priority over conflicting federal laws. Yes or no.[5]
  3. Question 10 · Separation. Remain, or start the process toward a binding separation referendum. A choice of two options. Not binding. This page is about this question only.[1]

How and when the vote happens

Advance voting runs Tuesday, Oct. 13, to Saturday, Oct. 17. On Monday, Oct. 19, voting places are open from 9 a.m. to 8 p.m., and electors vote in their own electoral division.[7] 174,532 people voted in person on the first day of advance voting.[8] Question 10 is counted first. Unofficial results start after 8 p.m. on Oct. 19, with Question 10 expected to be fully counted early on Oct. 20 and the other questions by Oct. 21. The count must be completed within 48 hours, and official results are scheduled for Oct. 29.[9][10][11]

Chapter 2 · The polls

Remain has led in every poll on the ballot question

Eleven public polls have asked the ballot question since the Premier announced it on May 21. Option 2 has ranged from 19% to 35% of all respondents, and each firm’s own results have changed little since May.

Chapter 3 · Timeline

How the question reached the ballot

Two citizen petitions, three court decisions and a cabinet order shaped what voters see on Oct. 19.

  1. 2025
  2. May 5
    Premier says separation will go on a ballot only through a citizen petition

    Danielle Smith says the government will not put separation on the referendum ballot itself, but a question would go to a vote if a citizen petition succeeds.[12]

  3. May 15
    Bill 54 makes citizen petitions easier

    The new rules need signatures equal to 10% of votes cast in the last election, collected over 120 days. They take effect July 4, 2025.[13][14]

  4. Summer
    A separation petition question goes to court

    The Chief Electoral Officer asks the Court of King’s Bench whether the Alberta Prosperity Project’s question, “Do you agree that the Province of Alberta shall become a sovereign country and cease to be a province of Canada?”, is constitutional.[15]

  5. Dec. 1
    Petition to remain in Canada is verified

    Thomas Lukaszuk’s “Forever Canadian” petition, “Do you agree that Alberta should remain in Canada?”, is verified with 404,293 signatures, above the 293,976 required (10% of registered electors). It goes to a legislative committee.[16][17]

  6. Dec. 4–5
    Bill 14 is introduced; the judge releases his opinion a day later

    The government introduces Bill 14, which would end the court case and remove the rule that petition questions be constitutional. The next day, before the bill takes effect, Justice Colin Feasby releases his opinion that the proposed question would breach the Constitution, including Treaty rights. He says the ruling does not stop Alberta from holding a separation referendum.[18][19][20]

  7. 2026
  8. Jan. 3
    A new separation petition begins

    Stay Free Alberta, linked to the Alberta Prosperity Project, starts collecting signatures for a referendum on independence. It needs 177,732 by May 2.[21]

  9. May 13
    Court quashes approval of the separation petition

    Justice Shaina Leonard rules that the province failed in its duty to consult First Nations before approving the petition. The province and the petitioners both appeal.[22]

  10. May 21
    Question 10 is added to the October ballot

    In a televised address, the Premier adds a separation question to the nine already planned for Oct. 19. The same day, a legislative committee recommends a referendum on the Forever Canadian question. Cabinet instead sets its own two-option question.[23][24]

  11. Late May
    Ottawa says the Clarity Act does not apply to this question

    Prime Minister Mark Carney says the pitch that a vote is a “free option” that will “strengthen your hand in future negotiation” is “a very dangerous bluff.” He later says the Clarity Act does not apply, because Question 10 asks about holding a later vote and is explicitly non-binding. Smith says the decision belongs to Albertans.[25][26][27]

  12. May 28
    Cabinet sets the wording

    Order in Council 160/2026 sets Question 10 and its two options, and says the result is not binding.[1]

  13. June 29
    Appeal court lets signature counting go ahead

    A partial stay lets Elections Alberta verify the Stay Free Alberta signatures but blocks the petition from going further until the appeal is decided.[28]

  14. July 24
    Separation petition reaches the signature threshold

    Elections Alberta estimates 222,597 valid signatures against 177,732 needed. The petition stays on hold while the appeal is pending.[29]

  15. Sept. 16
    Cost estimate released

    A University of Calgary School of Public Policy report, commissioned by the province and reviewed by an expert panel chaired by Jack Mintz, estimates the cost of setting up a new country at $50 billion to $170 billion in the first five years.[30]

  16. Oct. 9
    Premier rules out a second vote if Remain wins

    Asked about another separation referendum if Option 1 wins, Smith says, “Not as long as I’m premier.” NDP Leader Naheed Nenshi later makes the same commitment.[31][32]

  17. Oct. 13–17
    Advance voting

    174,532 people vote in person on the first day. More than 360,000 have voted in person after two days, and about 418,000 of the 658,000 requested mail-in ballots have been returned by Oct. 14.[8][33]

  18. Oct. 19
    Voting day

    Polls are open 9 a.m. to 8 p.m. Question 10 is counted first. Official results are scheduled for Oct. 29.[7][9][11]

Chapter 4 · What happens next

What each result would set in motion

Neither option changes Alberta’s status on Oct. 19. Leaving Canada would take a later referendum on secession itself, negotiations and a constitutional amendment.

If Option 1, Remain, gets more votes

Nothing changes in law. Smith and Nenshi have both said they would not hold another separation referendum. Smith said, “Not as long as I’m premier.”[31][32] Separatist leaders say their campaign will continue.[34]

The Stay Free Alberta petition is a separate route. It reached its signature threshold but is on hold until the Court of Appeal rules. A ruling in its favour could still lead to a citizen-initiated referendum.[28][29]

One law professor argues a Remain majority would legally bind the government, because the question is in substance a constitutional one. No court has tested this.[35]

If Option 2, Start the process, gets more votes

Alberta’s status does not change. The government would be politically committed, not legally bound, to begin a “legal process” that it has not spelled out. Smith has said that if Option 2 wins, “we’ll have to begin the legal process to get to a binding referendum.”[1][36]

Over the summer, Smith spoke of a binding referendum in early 2027, after about two and a half months of consultation with First Nations.[37] On Aug. 27 she said a provincial rule against holding a referendum within a year of a general election complicates that timing. The next election is set for Oct. 18, 2027.[38]

As of Oct. 15 she had not committed to a timeline.[32]

The steps between this vote and separation

In a 1998 reference opinion, the Supreme Court of Canada said a province cannot secede unilaterally. Separation would need an amendment to the Constitution, reached through negotiation, and a duty to negotiate arises only after a clear majority votes for separation on a clear question.[39] The federal Clarity Act sets out how the House of Commons judges both.[40]

  1. The Oct. 19 vote

    A non-binding choice between Remain and Start the process.

    Where it standsPoll average: Remain 75%, Start the process 25% of decided voters. Likely range for Option 2: 18% to 32%.

  2. The “legal process” begins

    The ballot does not define the process. Smith has spoken of consulting First Nations first.[37] The province argues in court that consultation is not required at this stage.[22]

    Where it standsA court ruled in May 2026 that the duty to consult applied to the earlier separation petition. The province is appealing.[22]

  3. A binding question is set, and Ottawa judges whether it is clear

    Under the Clarity Act, the House of Commons has 30 days after a province releases a secession question to decide whether it is clear. A question is not clear if it merely seeks a mandate to negotiate without asking directly whether the province should become independent, or if it combines secession with other possibilities, such as a new economic or political partnership with Canada, that obscure a direct choice.[40]

    Where it standsOttawa says the Act does not apply to Question 10. Carney has also said any separation attempt must follow the Clarity Act, whose test covers a question on secession itself.[27][41]

  4. A binding referendum

    A vote to leave must be a clear majority on a clear question. After the vote, the House of Commons decides whether that test was met. The law does not set a number.[39][40] Smith has said she treats 50 per cent plus one as a majority on Question 10. Prime Minister Carney told the House of Commons in May 2026 that a separation vote would need a clear majority, not just 50 per cent plus one.[3][42]

    Where it standsIn 2026 polls that asked directly how people would vote on separating, between 14% and 33% of respondents chose to separate. The 33% is from the Alberta part of a Canada-wide Research Co. survey (July 20–22); Alberta-only polls ranged from 14% to 30%. None of the polls in this inventory, going back to 2025, found a majority for leaving.

  5. Negotiations

    A clear result creates a duty for Ottawa, the provinces and Alberta to negotiate. The Clarity Act lists matters that must be addressed, including assets and debts, borders, the rights and claims of Indigenous peoples, and minority rights.[39][40]

    Where it standsTreaty 8 Grand Chief Trevor Mercredi has said there can be no decisions on separation without First Nations’ consultation and consent.[43] The province-commissioned cost study estimated $50 billion to $170 billion in transition costs in the first five years.[30]

  6. A constitutional amendment

    Separation needs an amendment to the Constitution. Experts disagree on which formula applies. One requires Parliament plus at least seven provinces with at least half the population of all the provinces; the other requires Parliament and every province.[44]

    Where it standsNo court has ruled on which formula applies. Only after an amendment would Alberta stop being a province.

Chapter 5 · The arguments

Each side’s main arguments, with the evidence its supporters cite

Five points for each option, about the same length, in ballot order. These are the campaigns’ claims and the evidence they point to. This page does not rate them.

Option 1 · Remain

Registered campaign groups include Forever Canadian (Thomas Lukaszuk), Vote to Stay (Monte Solberg) and Alberta’s Voice (Stephen Carter).[50][51][52]

Leaving would be costly

Remain supporters cite a study commissioned by the province and released Sept. 16, which estimates $50 billion to $170 billion in transition costs in the first five years. In a June 2026 online survey of 137 Calgary Chamber members, 48% said they were likely to relocate if the separation process moves forward.

Sources: University of Calgary School of Public Policy; Calgary Chamber survey by Probe Research[30][53]

Treaties and the law stand in the way

Treaty 8 Grand Chief Trevor Mercredi said there can be “no decisions about Alberta separating without consultation and consent of our First Nations.” In May a Court of King’s Bench judge found that Alberta’s secession “will have an impact on Treaties 7 and 8.” That ruling is under appeal by the province and the petitioners.

Sources: Treaty 8 First Nations of Alberta; Court of King’s Bench[43][54][28]

Alberta’s problems can be fixed inside Canada

Conservative Leader Pierre Poilievre told Albertans that “separation will not solve problems” and urged them to keep pressing Ottawa on oil and gas policy. Premier Smith has pointed to joint projects such as the Pacific Link pipeline to the B.C. coast as proof the country is better together.

Sources: Pierre Poilievre; Danielle Smith[55][56]

Option 2 is a step toward leaving, not just a message

Remain supporters note that Option 2’s text asks for a binding referendum on separation. Prime Minister Carney said the pitch that a vote is a “free option” that will “strengthen your hand in future negotiation” is “a very dangerous bluff.”

Source: Mark Carney[25]

Albertans identify with Canada

In an Angus Reid poll in May, a strong sense of Canadian identity was among the main reasons people gave for wanting to stay. The Forever Canadian petition, which asked whether Alberta should remain in Canada, was verified with 404,293 signatures.

Sources: Angus Reid Institute; Elections Alberta, as reported[57][16]

Option 2 · Start the process

Registered campaign groups include Let Alberta Decide (Keith Wilson and Tanya Clemens) and Pathway to Independence (Cory Morgan). The Alberta Prosperity Project (Mitch Sylvestre) also backs Option 2.[58][56][59]

A stronger hand with Ottawa

Keith Wilson of Let Alberta Decide argues that Option 2 would open talks with Ottawa that are otherwise unavailable, after which Albertans could weigh their options. He calls the other nine questions a “political gimmick” that would not bring real change without the leverage Option 2 provides.

Source: Keith Wilson, Let Alberta Decide[60]

Alberta sends more to Ottawa than it gets back

Option 2 supporters cite Alberta’s net contribution to federal finances, which the Fraser Institute puts at $244.6 billion from 2007 to 2022. In an Angus Reid poll in May, Alberta getting less from Canada than it gives was among the main reasons people gave for wanting to leave.

Sources: Fraser Institute; Angus Reid Institute[61][57]

Ottawa’s rules hold back Alberta energy

Option 2 supporters point to federal project-review laws, which they blame for blocked pipelines, and to oil sold mainly to one buyer, the United States, at a discount. In 2023 the Supreme Court of Canada found the main review scheme of the federal Impact Assessment Act unconstitutional. Cory Morgan of Pathway to Independence said a new pipeline “doesn’t change the system whatsoever.”

Sources: The Margin; Supreme Court of Canada; Cory Morgan[62][63][56]

Albertans have a right to decide

Supporters note that Elections Alberta estimated 222,597 valid signatures on the Stay Free Alberta petition, above the 177,732 needed, and that the petition is on hold while the courts decide whether First Nations had to be consulted first. Morgan told CBC News that promising never to hold another vote disregards direct democracy.

Sources: Elections Alberta, as reported; Cory Morgan[29][64]

Fixing it inside Canada hasn’t worked

UCP MLA Jason Stephan says he backs Option 2 because he wants Ottawa to be compelled to fix what is broken. In his newsletter he wrote that voting to remain “entrenches the status quo,” while a vote for a future referendum “says no to the status quo.”

Source: Jason Stephan[65]

Party leaders: the leaders of the UCP, the Alberta NDP, the federal Liberals and the federal Conservatives have all said they support remaining. The Republican Party of Alberta and the Wildrose Loyalty Coalition back Option 2, as does UCP MLA Jason Stephan.[66][32][25][55][67][65]

Chapter 6 · Method & sources

How the forecast is calculated

The poll average

Only polls that asked the ballot question feed the forecast. For each poll, undecided respondents are removed and the two options are scaled to add to 100.

Each firm’s latest poll counts once. Its weight combines how recent it is and how many people were asked:

weight = 0.5 ^ (days old ÷ 21) × √(sample ÷ 1,000)

A poll three weeks older counts half as much. Samples above 3,000 are capped, and a poll with no published sample size counts as 1,000. Polls paid for by a campaign on either side are listed but not averaged. One was found, for the Vote to Stay campaign in June 2026, and it did not ask the ballot question.

The likely range

Three sources of uncertainty are combined: sampling error, using a design effect of 1.5 for every poll; disagreement between firms; and how far final polls have missed in past referendums. The likely range covers about 8 outcomes in 10 if these assumptions hold. The wide range covers 19 in 20.

uncertainty = √(sampling² + spread² ÷ effective firms + past miss²) = √(0.8² + 3.0² ÷ 4.6 + 5.4²) ≈ 5.6 points

There are six firms, but the weights are unequal, so they count as 4.6 effective firms. Result for Option 2: 24.7% of decided voters. Likely range 17.5% to 31.9%; wide range 13.7% to 35.8%.

The chance that Option 2 tops 50% comes from the same calculation. A normal curve puts it far below 1%. Fatter-tailed curves (Student’s t with 3 to 5 degrees of freedom) put it at 0.3% to 1.0%, so the page shows “1% or less”.

The forecast includes no adjustment for a late swing. In past referendums, final polls have missed toward the status quo (Quebec 1995, Scotland 2014) and toward change (the UK in 2016, New Caledonia in 2018).

Checks

The centre barely moves with other reasonable choices. Option 2 comes out at 24.9% using every poll instead of each firm’s latest, at 24.1% or 25.4% with a 10- or 45-day half-life, and at 24.6% with no sample-size weight. Using Abacus Data’s leaners-allocated figures instead of its all-respondent figures would add about 0.2 points.

The range depends more on which past referendums are counted. New Caledonia 2018, whose pre-vote polls are poorly documented, supplies most of the past-miss term. Without it, the likely range for Option 2 would be about 20% to 29%. Adding Colombia’s 2016 peace-deal referendum, where polls overstated Yes by about 15 points, would widen it to about 14% to 35%. In that widest version, with the same fat-tailed curves, Option 2’s chance of winning would be about 1% to 3%.

How far final polls have missed

The “past miss” term is the root-mean-square of these five misses, about 5.4 points, using about 2.3 for Quebec and 10.5 for New Caledonia. Votes boycotted by one side, such as Catalonia in 2017, are left out because their polls cannot be compared with the results.

Limits

  • Most of these polls use online panels, which do not have a true margin of error. Firms report a comparable figure for reference only.
  • Turnout could change the result. Some commentators, including former premier Jason Kenney, say very low turnout would help Option 2.[74] Elections Alberta said it was preparing for a record turnout of about 85%, and more than 360,000 people voted in person in the first two days of advance voting.[75][33] Who turns out matters as much as how many. Ipsos found Remain supporters less motivated (Sept.), and Angus Reid found leave supporters more often certain to vote, 88% to 81% (Aug.). Léger found NDP voters, who overwhelmingly back Remain, more likely to vote than UCP voters, 93% to 78% (Oct.).[76][77][78]
  • In an Angus Reid poll of May 22–24, 2026, 51% called the question confusing.[79]
  • Polls released after Oct. 16 are not included. The newest ballot-question poll found ended fieldwork on Oct. 4.
  • Two other poll averages, built from largely the same polls, show similar figures. The Writ had Option 2 at 24% of decided voters on Oct. 9, and 338Canada had support for leaving under 25% on Oct. 11.[80][81]
  • Figures and quotes were gathered from published sources found through web searches on Oct. 16, 2026, and cross-checked where possible. Quotes that could not be confirmed in a published source were left out.
